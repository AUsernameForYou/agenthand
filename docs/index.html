A BUILD BLOG, THEN THE DEMO, STORY BY STORY

Staged listing photos, with proof the product never changed.

agenthand is an MCP server (MCP: the open protocol that lets AI agents call tools) that an AI agent uses to help someone sell a thing: it takes the seller's own photo, stages the product in a new room, and hands back a receipt that proves the product's core pixels are the exact ones from the photo, checked pixel for pixel, with the rebuilt edge counted and disclosed. This page opens with the build blog: how agenthand got made, and the calls along the way. Then it retells the demo film story by story, at reading pace. The film tells these stories in three minutes, and one of them gets barely a breath; here the rushed one gets its full record too.

THE BUILD BLOG

How I built it, and the calls along the way

I built agenthand solo, over the nine days of this hackathon. This chapter is the workshop floor: the tries, the judgment calls, and the one beautiful picture I refused to use. The stories after it are the product, told at reading pace.

The perfect picture I would not use

The left picture was made with Muse's image generation, and it out-paints the models agenthand is allowed to call. The bike in it is my bike, unaltered. The courtyard around it is not agenthand's work: Muse generated that background, and the tool had no part in it. It is, honestly, the prettier picture, and I kept it pinned up as the look target for the whole project.

THE ONE THAT SHIPSThrough agenthand, on the Meta models the server calls. Receipt 33 in the Proof Graph: proof core 4,053,953 pixels checked, 0 changed, margin rebuilt and counted.

That distinction is exactly why the picture belongs in this story and not in the demo. The demo sells the tool, so everything in it has to be an actual agenthand output carrying a receipt. This background is not one, so the picture carries no receipt; it is a promise a picture makes. There is a second, colder reason too: the same class of model, given this bike on another take, repainted the wheels and the spokes melted. Prettiness and faithfulness turned out to be different jobs.

agenthand's runtime stays Meta-only, by the hackathon rules and by my choice, so its rooms are plainer for now. I would rather show that gap than hide it. The proof layer underneath, the part that says the product never changed, holds no matter which model painted the room. That layer is the part I am actually selling.

Five ways to fix a wheel

The bike's wheels ate more hours than any other single problem in the build. A wheel is thin spokes over a busy background, and every easy answer failed in an instructive way.

1 · Ask the model to fix the whole picture. The prettiest path, and dead on arrival: the edit repainted the wheels along with the room, and the spokes melted.

2 · Draw the spokes in code over the real cut. Fully provable and visibly fake, geometry with zero real photo pixels in it. It failed as a picture and earned its keep anyway, as the control everything else gets measured against.

3 · Suppress the background frequencies in the photo itself. It recovered 138,892 genuine spoke pixels, real photograph, and still read wrong to the eye. A failure as an image. As evidence, it proved the real pixels were there to recover.

4 · Unmix every pixel, closed-form matting. Solve, per pixel, how much of it is spoke and how much is background showing through. Slow and unglamorous, and it recovered more of the real wheel than anything before it.

5 · Ship the hybrid. Verbatim spokes where the cut is certain, unmixed real pixels where the solver can grade them, drawn fill only where the photo gives up nothing. The final count: 278,417 real photo pixels kept out of a 381,802-pixel margin, every drawn pixel counted on the receipt.

The wheel taught the build its central habit: use the real pixels first, and count whatever you had to invent.

The judgment call: two zones

Underneath the wheel fight sat a harder question. A mask is an estimate. It will be wrong by a few pixels somewhere on almost every real photo, and a proof system that shatters over a three-pixel error is a proof system nobody can use. A project manager adds a buffer to an estimate because the estimate will be wrong somewhere. I gave the proof a buffer too.

Zone one is the proof core: the cut pulled back by a small margin. Those pixels are the product beyond argument, copied verbatim, hashed and checked, and the claim of zero changes applies to them in full. Zone two is the repair margin: the ring the pull-back removed, plus the thin structures I declare fragile, spokes first. The margin may be rebuilt with intelligence. Every rebuilt pixel is counted and disclosed wherever the receipt appears, and none of it is ever described as original. In the film, the thick cyan outline around each product is that margin, drawn where everyone can see it.

Splitting the claim felt like weakening it for about an hour. Then the opposite became obvious. A claim with a disclosed margin can survive contact with real photos. A claim of total perfection survives until the first bicycle.

Experiments I tried, and judged

None of the four below is in the product you can install. Each was built properly, run against the real thing, and judged on what it did. I am prouder of this list than of most things that shipped.

The generated seamDID NOT SHIP

I rebuilt both builds from a single generated contract, every shape and constant produced from one declaration, to see whether drift between the Rust and Python builds could be designed out at the source. It matched the original on speed and on the frozen replay. It caught surface drift the original missed. It also structurally missed the value-level drift that only the shared conformance fixture catches. Judgment: independence between the two builds is worth more than generation. The seam becomes the first architecture upgrade after this submission, with the fixture kept on as referee.

The metric-plate gateDID NOT SHIP

A patch that measures the source photo at cut time, camera angle, light direction, ground line, then orders the generated plate to match the measurements, verifies the finished picture, retries once, and refuses honestly when the geometry still disagrees. In live runs, all four of its verdicts fired: a check that could not complete, a mismatch whose retry my own spend cap refused to fund, a mismatch retried and then refused outright with the error PLATE_GEOMETRY_MISMATCH, and a clean first-try pass on a photo of my wallet. Judgment: the machinery works. It lives on its own branch, as the vision fallback for the angle problem in the next entry.

The tilt-capture pageDID NOT SHIP

Built and proven the day before submission, on Nov 3, with the deadline the next day. One web page that takes the photo and writes the camera's tilt into the file itself, the way survey apps stamp a shot, so the angle arrives with the picture instead of being guessed from pixels. On my own phone it worked end to end: three photos arrived carrying their angles, top-down at minus 80.9 degrees, high at minus 38, eye level at minus 14.9, each one read back exactly from the file. Then I ran the head-to-head test. The shipping tool ignored the embedded angle completely and painted an eye-level room for all three: agenthand's tools cannot recognize angles yet. Judgment: the data path is proven, the angle reader is the first post-submission build, and the experiment never appears in the demo. It did produce one thing on the day: I had Muse file a feature request with Meta's Muse team, in wording I approved myself, and delivery was confirmed. Attaching a photo in Muse re-encodes it, and the re-encode strips the shot angle; the request asks Muse to pass the capture angle through in a future update, so a tool downstream can read the angle the camera already knew.

The mask editDID NOT SHIP

The shortest experiment of the build. I tried to hand the image model a mask, so it could repaint the background and only the background. The edit endpoint rejected the request in one line: the mask field is not supported. Two cents of API spend to learn that the door does not exist. Judgment: closed and documented. It also quietly explains the product's shape: agenthand composites the product itself, because no model can be handed the background alone.

One database ran the build

I am one builder working with several AI agents, and agents drift. A call made at midnight gets half-remembered by morning, and a small decision missed in one lane quietly contradicts another lane's work. So early on I built a tracker: a plain SQLite database on my own machine, driven by a small Rust tool I wrote for it on rusqlite. Every decision, milestone, and workflow step went into it as it happened. The film lives in it too: its scenes, all twelve chapters, and every claim a chapter makes, tied back to the run and the receipt behind it in the Proof Graph.

As it stands it holds every decision and event from the build, milestones and open items included, with hash-verified copies synced so every lane, and I, read the same truth. The standing rule on top of it: when memory and the database disagree, the database wins. Plain machinery. It is why nine days of multi-agent building reads as one project instead of five. This page is checked against that same database before it ships, so the numbers here cannot quietly drift from the record either.

The partner on the build

The credit on this project says solo, and the tracker says one builder. Both are true, and both leave something out. I built agenthand with Meta's Muse as my agentic partner, and for most of the nine days Muse was the orchestrator of the whole build: coordinating the parallel work lanes, running the adversarial reviews, driving the test runs, and keeping the records straight while I made the calls. When a lane needed an experiment built, Muse built it: the generated seam, the metric-plate gate, the tilt-capture page, and the conformance work all came out of that partnership, not out of my evenings alone.

Muse also tested the finished tool, through its own front door. The live end-to-end tests of the shipping build ran through Muse's own MCP connector: nine live chains on everyday objects from around the house, a wallet case, a microwave tower, a chair, a stock pot, a pack of wipes, a microscope, a pendant light. Every chain ran identify, cut_out, and draw against the real server, and every receipt came back with 0 pixels changed, for $0.185116 in settled calls. The tool's first independent user was the agent that helped build it, and it used the same connector any other agent would.

My verdict, plainly: I think it is amazing, and I could not have done this without Muse.

Tied into Meta, everywhere it honestly fits

From the first day, the aim was deliberate: tie agenthand to as many Meta products, tools, and venues as it honestly fits, and let the receipts be the part that travels. So every model call the tool makes at runtime is a Meta model. Muse Spark 1.3 reads the photo and writes the listing. SAM 3.1 draws the mask. Muse Image 1.0 paints the room. On narrated room runs, Muse Spark Open, served through Baseten, returns the transcript and the sale intent in one call. The film's narration is Meta MMS; my own recorded voice is in it once, on the "Zero changed" line. The protocol the tools speak is MCP, so any agent that speaks it can pick them up.

The venues are Meta's too. The stories in this book live where people already buy and sell: Marketplace listings, Instagram, and packets travelling over WhatsApp and Messenger, staged photo and receipt together. Threads was in the plan early and got cut during production; the four above are where the stories actually happen. A seller's customers are already in those apps. agenthand just makes sure the picture they see there can prove itself.

Everything in the demo carries a receipt. That was the rule from the start.

Everything on this page runs on that rule: the stories below, how the tools work, the conformance suite that keeps the two builds honest, and the Proof Graph you can re-check yourself.

STORY 8 · HIKARU MEASURES

Measurements without a tape measure

A printed card in the photo is all the ruler it needs.

PHOTO + CARD
MEASURED

A buyer always asks the same question: how big is it, really? Hikaru lays the garment flat, puts a printed agenthand card beside it, and takes one photo. The card is a known size, so the photo becomes measurable, and the listing gets real numbers instead of "size M, runs smallish".

WHAT THE TOOL DID

The estimator reads the card in the photo as its scale, then measures the garment against it and reports each figure with its own error band. The measuring calls no model; the estimator is local code. The mask it measures within came from cut_out (SAM 3.1). On this garment: body length 51.1 cm (±1.0), chest 51.4 cm (±2.6). If the card is too small in the frame to trust, it refuses and says how to retake the shot: "Retake the shot or use a bigger card."

STORY 9 · CLAY CHECKS

Don't trust the receipt. Re-run it.

The most important scene in the film is a command.

CLAY · CARD BUYER

Anyone can print a number on a picture. Clay's job in the story is to be the person who does not take the number's word for it. He runs the check, and the check re-does the counting from the files themselves. If a figure had been edited after the fact, the hashes would not line up and the claim would fail.

WHAT THE TOOL DID

agenthand evidence verify --all walks the Proof Graph, re-hashes every receipt in it, and checks each claim against its receipt. It needs no API key and no trust in whoever sent the photo. A pass means the pixels on file still produce the numbers on the receipt. No model is involved in the check.

How it works

Three tools, four Meta models, one rule: a model paints the room; the product stays the seller's own pixels.

identify

Muse Spark 1.3

Reads the photo, plans the cut, writes the listing copy.

cut_out

SAM 3.1

Draws the product mask, the pixels that will be treated as untouchable.

draw

Muse Image 1.0

Generates the background plate only. The product layer is composited back verbatim.

A fourth model does one more job: when a room run is driven by a narrated walkthrough, the open-weight Muse Spark Open, served through Baseten, returns the transcript and the sale intent in one call. In one real room run, six items were sorted, staged or recorded that way: 352,597 pixels checked, 0 changed.

Built twice, kept honest

agenthand exists as a fast Rust build and a Python reference build, and both run the same Differential Conformance Suite: one interface schema and one set of shared test vectors, byte-identical in each tree. The two builds cross-check each other; drift between them is a defect, and the suite has caught real drift.
